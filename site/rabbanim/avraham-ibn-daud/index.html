ר׳ אברהם אבן דאוד הלוי

Avraham Ibn Daud
1110–1180
Follow this story on the map

ר׳ אברהם אבן דאוד הלוי (c. 1110–c. 1180), usually distinguished as הראב״ד הראשון, was an Andalusian Jewish historian, philosopher and astronomer whose mature career unfolded in Toledo. He was a grandson of ר׳ יצחק אבן אלבאליה and was educated in Córdoba in the household of his maternal uncle ר׳ ברוך בן יצחק אבן אלבאליה, a dayyan and academy head. That formation combined rabbinics, Bible and Hebrew poetry with the Arabic philosophical and scientific curriculum, especially the works of al-Farabi and Ibn Sina. Following the Almohad conquest of Córdoba and the persecution or displacement of non-Muslim communities in the late 1140s, he moved north into Christian Castile and settled in Toledo. There, around 1160–1161, he produced two complementary defenses of rabbinic Judaism. ספר הקבלה used historical succession to argue for the unbroken authority of the Oral Torah against Karaite claims; its unusually rich accounts of the Geonim and medieval Spanish Jewry made it a foundational work of Jewish historiography even where its stories require critical reading. His Judeo-Arabic אלעקידה אלרפיעה, preserved in Hebrew as האמונה הרמה and האמונה הנשאה, used philosophy to reconcile revelation with reason and to address substance, matter and form, the soul, God, prophecy, free will and ethics. It was the first systematic presentation of Aristotelian natural philosophy and metaphysics within Jewish philosophy, drawing particularly on Ibn Sina. The larger historical project now called דורות עולם also included chronicles of the Second Temple kings and Rome and a midrash on Zechariah. He refers to an anti-Karaite work, a medieval source credits him with astronomy, and an Arabic fragment of a work on physics has been identified. Scholars have long debated whether he was identical with Avendauth, the Jewish philosopher who collaborated with Dominicus Gundissalinus on Arabic-to-Latin translations of Ibn Sina in Toledo; recent research strengthens the identification, but it remains unproved and is therefore not stated as certain here. An early thirteenth-century Toledan source reports that he died a martyr around 1180, without preserving reliable details of the event. His work forms a crucial bridge between Andalusian Jewish learning, the Toledo translation culture and the mature Jewish Aristotelianism associated with ר׳ משה בן מימון, though ר׳ משה בן מימון never names him explicitly.
