ר׳ יצחק בן יהודה אבן גיאת

Yitzhak Ibn Ghayyat
1038–1089
Follow this story on the map

ר׳ יצחק בן יהודה אבן גיאת, known by the abbreviation ריצ״ג, was one of the leading halakhists, Talmud commentators, biblical exegetes and liturgical poets of eleventh-century al-Andalus. Born in Lucena in 1038 into an established local Jewish family, he helped make the city—already renowned as al-Yusana, the 'city of the Jews'—a central academy of Andalusian Torah. ר׳ שמואל הנגיד and his son ר׳ יהוסף הנגיד esteemed and supported him. He composed an Aramaic elegy after ר׳ שמואל's death in 1055/56 and two elegies after ר׳ יהוסף was killed in Granada in 1066. When ר׳ יהוסף's widow and young son ר׳ עזריה escaped to Lucena after the massacre, ר׳ יצחק sheltered and supported them and hoped the son might eventually assume communal leadership; those hopes ended when ר׳ עזריה died young. From 1066 ר׳ יצחק served as rabbi and head of the Lucena academy, combining communal judgment, teaching, responsa and authorship. His students included ר׳ משה אבן עזרא, ר׳ יוסף אבן סהל and ר׳ יוסף אבן צדיק; his son ר׳ יהודה בן יצחק אבן גיאת also continued the family's scholarly and poetic activity. His halakhic method stands at the transition from Geonic responsa and monographs to the emerging Andalusian codes. The work now printed as שערי שמחה or מאה שערים is a surviving part of a larger compendium probably called הלכות כלולות. Its extant festival and mourning laws preserve extensive Geonic rulings and sources otherwise lost, while also organizing them for practical use. שערי שמחה is the editor ר׳ יצחק דב במברגר's nineteenth-century title, not demonstrably the author's own. ר׳ יצחק also wrote a Judaeo-Arabic Talmud commentary called כתאב אלסראג (ספר הנר); fragments and citations reveal close use of ר׳ חננאל בן חושיאל and Geonic traditions. His כתאב אלזהד, a Judaeo-Arabic translation and philosophical-exegetical commentary on Ecclesiastes, examines asceticism, wisdom and the proper use of worldly life. Further biblical explanations, Talmudic interpretations and responsa survive in quotations and manuscripts, but reports of complete commentaries to many books should be treated cautiously. More than three hundred—and by some counts roughly four hundred—of his sacred poems survive. His selihot, requests, yoẓer compositions, qerovot, wedding poems, elegies and Yom Kippur Avodah incorporate biblical and rabbinic language alongside philosophy and science, contributing to the development of Hebrew as a vehicle for learned religious poetry. Many entered North African and Mediterranean rites, particularly collections known as שפתי רננות. Older literature variously calls him teacher, colleague or opponent of ר׳ יצחק אלפסי. The chronology makes simple teacher-pupil formulas unreliable: ר׳ יצחק אלפסי was substantially older and succeeded him at Lucena only after 1089. The safest conclusion is that they belonged to intersecting but distinct halakhic traditions and sometimes disagreed. In 1089, seriously ill at age fifty-one, ר׳ יצחק traveled from Lucena to nearby Córdoba for medical care. He died there on the Sabbath; his body was carried back overnight and buried with his ancestors in Lucena. His academy then passed to ר׳ יצחק אלפסי, strengthening Lucena's role as a bridge from Geonic North Africa to the classical scholarship of the Spanish Rishonim.
