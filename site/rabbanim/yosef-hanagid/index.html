ר׳ יהוסף בן שמואל הלוי הנגיד

Yosef HaNagid
1035–1066
Follow this story on the map

ר׳ יהוסף בן שמואל הלוי הנגיד (15 September 1035–30 December 1066), known in Arabic as Abū Ḥusayn Yūsuf ibn Naghrīla, was a scholar, literary editor, communal leader and vizier in Zirid Granada. He was the eldest son of ר׳ שמואל הלוי בן יוסף הנגיד and grew up at the centre of a court that joined Hebrew learning with Arabic literary and administrative culture. His father supervised an exacting education and, when ר׳ יהוסף was eight and a half, commissioned him to collect and organize the paternal Hebrew dīwān. ר׳ יהוסף copied poems, supplied Arabic headings and biographical notices, and continued the project for roughly twelve years; this editorial work preserves exceptional information about both men. In 1049 he married the only daughter of ר׳ נסים בן יעקב of Kairouan, joining two of the most influential scholarly families of the western Islamic world. A letter describing him as ר׳ נסים's pupil has been attributed to him, but both the attribution and any resulting claim that he personally studied in Kairouan remain uncertain. When ר׳ שמואל died in 1055 or 1056, the young ר׳ יהוסף succeeded him as vizier to Bādīs ibn Ḥabbūs and as nagid of Granada's Jews. He directed an academy, supported scholars such as ר׳ יצחק בן ברוך אבן אלבאליה and ר׳ יצחק אבן גיאת, and received dedications, including an astronomical-calendar work from ר׳ יצחק אבן אלבאליה in 1065. His decade in power is reconstructed from sharply conflicting testimony. Jewish sources emphasize his learning and patronage; Arabic court histories portray him as overmighty, secretive and politically ruthless. The later memoir of the Zirid ruler ʿAbd Allāh ibn Buluggīn accuses him of manipulating King Bādīs and conspiring with al-Muʿtaṣim of Almería. These allegations reflect genuine factional conflict but come through a dynastic narrative with its own agenda and cannot all be independently verified. Hostility to a Jewish vizier exercising authority over Muslims also had an explicitly anti-Jewish dimension. The jurist-poet Abū Isḥāq al-Ilbīrī circulated a poem attacking both ר׳ יהוסף and Granada's Jews, invoking their wealth and public office as violations of the proper social order. On 30 December 1066, amid a political crisis, a Muslim crowd stormed the royal palace, found and killed ר׳ יהוסף, publicly displayed his body, and then attacked the wider Jewish population. Sources agree that the communal killing was extensive, but the often-repeated figure of four thousand dead or fifteen hundred families cannot be independently confirmed and should not be mapped as an exact count. His wife—ר׳ נסים בן יעקב's daughter—and their young son ר׳ עזריה escaped to Lucena; the child later died young. The episode was both a court revolution and anti-Jewish collective violence: reducing it to either personal misconduct or timeless religious hatred obscures the interaction of dynastic faction, social hierarchy, polemic and minority vulnerability in eleventh-century Granada.
