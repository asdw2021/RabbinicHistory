ר׳ יוסף אבן אביתור

Yosef Ibn Avitur
940–1025

ר׳ יוסף בן יצחק אבן שטנאש אבן אביתור (active second half of the tenth and beginning of the eleventh century; mapped conventionally c. 940–c. 1025) was an Andalusian Talmudist, halakhic authority, biblical exegete and one of the most prolific early Hebrew liturgical poets of Muslim Spain. He often signed יוסף הספרדי or יוסף המרידי, reflecting his birth in Mérida, and explained that the family name אבן שטנאש derived from a powerful ancestor remembered as a scourge of wrongdoers. He moved to Córdoba and studied with ר׳ משה בן חנוך, becoming one of the academy's outstanding pupils. When ר׳ משה died around 965, the office passed to his son ר׳ חנוך בן משה with the support of ר׳ חסדאי אבן שפרוט. After ר׳ חסדאי's death, ר׳ יוסף pressed his own claim; Córdoba divided into factions, caliphal authority supported ר׳ חנוך, and ר׳ יוסף and his adherents were placed under a ban. He left al-Andalus and traveled through North Africa and the eastern Mediterranean. Later narrative sources say that a scholar in Fez refused to receive him and that a Babylonian Gaon also upheld the ban; the traditional identification of that Gaon as ר׳ האי גאון is chronologically problematic for the earliest dating of the dispute. Modern Encyclopaedia Judaica places him in Babylonia, the Land of Israel and Egypt, where he enjoyed the friendship of ר׳ שמואל הכהן, Gaon of the Palestinian yeshiva, and ר׳ שמריה בן אלחנן of Fustat. When ר׳ יעקב אבן ג׳ו later gained authority over Andalusian Jewish appointments, he removed ר׳ חנוך and invited ר׳ יוסף back to Córdoba. ר׳ יוסף refused and reportedly declared that no scholar from Spain to Babylonia was more worthy than his former rival, a striking reconciliation preserved in the later tradition. His eastern career was intellectually productive. ספר הקבלה reports that in Egypt he prepared an Arabic rendering or epitome connected with the six orders of rabbinic law for the Fatimid caliph al-Ḥākim; the work does not survive, and calling it a complete translation of the Babylonian Talmud overstates the evidence. Responsa attributed to him show substantial halakhic authority, although manuscript research demonstrates that some answers once assigned to other Geonim or Andalusian rabbis require reattribution. His greatest surviving corpus is poetry: hundreds—and in the broader manuscript record potentially more than a thousand—piyyutim across nearly every major liturgical genre, including a pioneering ma'amad, an elaborate סדר עבודה for Yom Kippur, festival compositions, hoshanot, selihot, qedushta cycles and historical laments. Modern scholarship also attributes to him biblical commentary, though its remains and boundaries are incomplete. He was still active after the persecutions of 1021, for which he composed a lament, and later tradition places his death in Damascus at an unknown date.
